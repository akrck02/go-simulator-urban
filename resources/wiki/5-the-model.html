🏘️ The urban growth model

Old towns grew in a recognisable way. People arrived; first they built along the roads; the houses filled their plots, back and then up; when there was no room left, new streets opened; busy crossings became squares; and one day the town was big and rich enough to build a wall. The urban plugin follows that story year by year, with rules taken from studies of real historic towns.

The example town: from 300 to 6,000 people in 100 years. It follows the roads and the river, and raises its first wall at year 60.

Every rule comes from a published model; the only rule of its own is the walls’, because no published model simulates them. It knows nothing about any particular city: the scenario says where, how many people and when.

The other pictures in this page show a second made-up town, a river town that grows from 600 to 36,000 people in 100 years; its whole rules file is in getting started.

The year

Each year, the town asks itself one question: do the people fit? The city rule sets up a city at the start and opens every year. Its instance name names the city; the other rules find it by their city parameter, so several cities can grow in one simulation. Rules run in the order of the rules file, usually:

  1. Demand. The population follows its curve (logistic: from from people in from_year to to in to_year, towards a ceiling). The floor space the people need is inhabitants × square_metres_per_person; the deficit is what is missing.
  2. Densify (Conzen 1960; Caniggia and Maffei). Before the city spreads, its plots fill up: each year up to a quarter of the plots advance, the most wanted first. The house first takes the back of the plot (coverage up to 0.7, or the house depth the scenario gives) and then gains a floor, up to the year’s maximum (new houses start with one floor, or two when the year allows three or more, and never more than the outskirts allow). rare_floors is a quota: at most its share of the plots may stand that high. Plots already that high keep their place in it and the most wanted of the rest fill what is left, so the share does not creep up as the town’s most wanted places move. In a dense core, a full plot builds at the back, round a yard.
  3. Expand (Weber et al. 2009). What is still missing goes to new plots along the most wanted free frontages: close to the growth centres, on gentle ground (no plot steeper than 0.4), inside the window, out of obstacles and water. A frontage less wanted than open_streets_below waits: a street nearby opens first. When no frontage is left, new streets grow from the network, along a tensor field (Chen et al. 2008) that follows the contours, the roads and the river; organic streets wander, continue from their ends and close into blocks.
  4. Walls (the plugin’s own rule). The first wall goes up when the population reaches first_wall.population or, at the latest, in first_wall.year. It follows the concave hull of what is built (or the given wall_outline), tied to the supporting rock when there is one, with gates where roads and main streets cross it (published as gate points) and a clear strip inside. When the suburbs outside hold more than new_wall_when_outside of the floor space and the wall is at least 12 years old, a new one goes up and the old one becomes a ring road with a belt of gardens and convents (Whitehand 1967). Its height can rise over the years (wall_rise) or follow the calendar (wall_heights).
  5. Prune empty streets. Dead ends nobody built on for grace_years go away.
  6. Split large blocks (Siksna 1997). An alley crosses blocks wider than maximum_side.
  7. Space syntax (Hillier and Iida 2005). Angular segment analysis over the street network, on samples origins within local_radius: the most chosen streets become main streets, the most central square the market, and plots on them shops.
  8. Squares. Irregular squares open at busy crossings, inside gates and at bridgeheads, one for every per_inhabitants people, at least spacing apart.
  9. Indicators. In the listed years, an indicators event with the measures of the city’s form.

After every request the plugin publishes the city once (AfterStep): only the entities that changed since the last time travel to the host.

Plots fill up, then the town spreads

A plot is a burgage: a narrow front on a street and a long depth behind it. Densify works on the plots that are already there (steps above); expand lays new ones along free frontages, and only opens streets when the frontages left are not wanted enough. With open_streets_below: 0.3 a town stays a street village along its roads; with 0.5 to 0.7 it opens back streets sooner and grows compact. In the river town (0.5) the first decades are ribbons along the roads and the quays; the back streets come later, when the frontages near the centre run out.

Walls and the ring road

The river town reaches 4,000 people, its first_wall.population, in year 33, and the wall goes up round what is built. By year 49 too much of the floor space lies outside, so a new wall goes up and the old line becomes a ring road; new plots in a belt along it are large gardens and convents (use extensive), and the town fills the room between the two walls.

Where the city grows

  • Relief: the world’s elevation field (put the terrain plugin first), or a relief file. Slopes steer streets along the contours and keep plots off steep ground. When the ground comes from the world and changes (the terrain’s erosion rule moves it), the city takes it again at the start of the next year.
  • Growth centres pull the city; a centre can start pulling in a year and grow in weight over some years, so a city gains districts as it grows. The growth radius widens with the population (the settled area goes as N^0.714, its radius as N^0.357).
  • Grid zones lay planned streets on a lattice from a year on.
  • Density falls from the core to the outskirts: narrow fronts, deep houses and more floors in the core; wide plots with gardens and lower houses outside. Districts can set their own density.

Streets

Every street piece has a class (see outputs). Roads come with the inputs; quays line the river; a ring follows a wall that was replaced; alleys split large blocks; the rest are streets the town opened. Space syntax turns the most chosen streets and alleys into main streets. In the river town, the three roads carry most of the movement, so only two pieces of the streets it opened become main streets.

Rivers

Rivers come as river features: a line along the axis with a width (km); their water is an obstacle. With the scenario’s river settings they shape the town: streets align with the banks, quays line both banks once the town reaches them, plots by the water are wanted and some become workshops (mills, tanners), and bridges are spaced and square to the river. The river is published as a river entity and quays as streets of class quay.

With river: {from_world: true} the town also takes the rivers the world already has, such as the ones the terrain’s erosion rule finds where the water gathers, so the town and the ground agree on where the water runs. They are taken once, when the city starts: a town does not move its quays and bridges after a river. Only the parts near the window count, and narrowest (km) leaves out the streams too narrow to shape a town.

Clearing the drawn scene

When the drawn scene already has something where the city grows (an old town, a forest), a clear feature marks it: at the start the plugin sends a clear_area for the scenario’s clear_nodes (holes in the feature are spared), and every year it clears the tree_nodes under the new plots.

Requirements

GEOS, the geometry engine shapely uses: sudo apt install libgeos-dev, sudo dnf install geos-devel or brew install geos. While it is not installed, a .geos-local/env.sh (ignored by git) can point cgo at an unpacked copy: . .geos-local/env.sh before building. More in getting started.
